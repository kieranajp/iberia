Iberia, scored

We live here. So where should we actually go?

A year of trips to plan, a whole peninsula to choose from, and four things we care about. Rather than guess, we put provinces across Spain and Portugal through the same four questions and let the map answer. The last question turned out to decide everything.

Can we eat?

Somewhere a vegetarian isn't handed a plate of ham.

Is there wine?

Proper wine country, not a supermarket shelf.

Will it rain on us?

A thunderstorm is fine. A fortnight of drizzle is not.

Will it be full of Brits?

We did not move here to queue behind a stag do.

Before the answers

Where these numbers came from

Two of the four are measurements. Two are opinions. It matters which is which, so here it is up front.

Measured

The rain

Ten years of daily weather for every province on this map, in squares about 55 km across, from the European reanalysis the forecasters check themselves against. It took the best part of two hours to download, one batch at a time, because the weather service kept telling us to slow down.

Measured

The wine

Spain's agriculture ministry and Portugal's equivalent between them publish every protected denomination — of them show up somewhere on this map — with real boundaries rather than dots on a map. We counted the ones whose territory falls inside each province.

Judged

The food

Province by province, what each one actually cooks — the dishes, not the restaurant listings — rated twice: once for how hard you'd find it without meat at all, and once for how well it does white fish and chicken. The first rating leads and decides who is ruled out; the second can lift a province one band and no further. There is no survey that answers either. The obvious substitute, counting vegetarian restaurants, turns out to measure where map-makers live rather than where you can eat.

Judged

The British bit

resorts and cities, rated by hand from 0 to 5. Spain's statistics office knows how many Britons visit the country, and it knows how many people visit each region, but it does not publish the two together — so there is nothing to check this against. Treat it as an argument, not a fact.

Question one

Can we eat?

The meat belt is real and it runs right across the middle: Castilla y León roasts lamb and suckling pig, Extremadura exists for the acorn-fed pig, Asturias puts pork in the bean stew, and central Portugal roasts its own piglet for Bairrada. The Mediterranean arc is a different country — rice, aubergines, artichokes, chickpeas.

White fish and chicken widen the field, so a fish-forward province is scored more kindly here than a strict reading would allow. They are a fallback, not a rescue: they lift a province by at most one band, and never save one. Somewhere that answers "something vegetarian?" with a plate of jamón is not going to be good at chicken either.

Question two

Is there wine?

Tarragona alone carries five denominations, Priorat and Montsant among them; Zaragoza has four; Ciudad Real has three. Portugal counts too now — Douro carries two of its own. This counts territory, not quality — five in Tarragona beats one in Rioja here, which is a conversation for the dinner table rather than a flaw in the map.

Question three

Will it rain on us?

Counting days it rained at all, not how much fell — because that is the difference between a dramatic August evening and a ruined week. Murcia gets 59 wet days a year. Navarra gets 157, and its vegetables are wonderful precisely because of it.

Question four

Will it be full of Brits?

Here is the decision that shapes everything after it. Being surrounded by Britons doesn't cost a trip a few points — it changes what the trip is. So this isn't a quarter of the score. It's a discount applied to the whole thing: every point of average British presence knocks 18% off whatever a province earned elsewhere.

Adding it up

The best places lose the most

Hatching is what each province earned on food, wine and weather; solid is what survives the discount. Málaga and Cádiz sit on the same coast with the same merit, 74, and the same food score, 88. One finishes on 30. The other finishes on 61. The difference is entirely who else goes there — Fuengirola, Marbella and Nerja against Conil and Zahara. It's why this ranking runs by province rather than by region: as a single Andalucía, those two would have averaged into meaninglessness. Tarragona has the highest merit anywhere in the ranking, 85, and Salou alone drags it down to 50.

The answer

The market garden of Europe: 59 wet days a year, artichokes and pisto on every menu, and nothing whatsoever that a tour operator wants. It wins by having nothing anyone else came for — Huelva has better food but only one wine denomination to Murcia's two, Jaén has no wine at all, and Cádiz has both but pays for the resorts on its own coast. Before you book

What not to trust here

In order of how much it should worry you.

No data reads the same as no Britons

of ranked provinces — % of them — have no place rated for British presence at all, so they clear question four by default rather than by evidence. Huelva and Jaén are both top five partly on that basis.

The British ratings are mine

Hand-compiled judgements about places, and the single biggest lever in the whole thing. If you think Sitges belongs at 3 rather than 2, the ranking changes.

Portugal is in, its British data mostly isn't

of Portugal's provinces have no rated resort at all — only do. The food and wine numbers are real; the Brit-free score there is mostly nobody having looked.

The weather is an average

Ten years, in 55 km squares. Enough to answer "is it grim in Galicia", useless for choosing a fortnight in May.

Wine is counted, not tasted

A denomination is a denomination here, Spanish or Portuguese, whether it's Priorat or somewhere you've never heard of with nine hectares.

And the whole thing is one opinion

Four weights, chosen by us, for us. Someone who eats meat and likes a familiar pub would get a completely different map out of the same data.
